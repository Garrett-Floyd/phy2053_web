Theorem 18.2.7.

For an object undergoing circular motion, the radial component of acceleration is
\begin{equation*} a_{r} = - \frac{v^{2}}{r} \end{equation*}
🔗
🔗

Proof.

The acceleration of an object undergoing circular motion can be broken down into a tangential component \(a_{t}\) which is the projection of \(\vec{a}\) onto the \(\hat{\theta}\) direction and a radial component \(a_{r}\) which is the projection of \(\vec{a}\) onto the \(\hat{r}\) direction.
🔗
Figure x shows the change in position of an object undergoing circular motion over a very short period of time \(\Delta t\text{.}\) Since the period of time \(\Delta t\) is so small we can approximate the acceleration as constant over this interval. During that period of time the displacements in the \(\hat{\theta}\) and \(\hat{r}\) directions must add up to keep the object on the circle. \(\vec{v}\) and \(a_{t}\hat{\theta}\) point in the \(\hat{\theta}\) direction, so according to Theorem 4.1.2 the total displacment in the \(\hat{\theta}\) direction is
\begin{equation*} \frac{1}{2}a_{t} \left( \Delta t \right)^{2} + v \Delta t. \end{equation*}
Since the \(\hat{\theta}\) is tangent to the circle, this displacement tries to get the object to fly off the circle in a straight line.
🔗
To keep the object from flying off, we must have the radial displacement be in the \(-\hat{r}\) direction (towards the center of the circle) to keep the object on circle. \(a_{r}\) is the projection of \(\vec{a}\) unto the positive \(\hat{r}\) direction. To relate \(a_{r}\) to the projection of \(\vec{a}\) unto the \(-\hat{r}\) direct we note that
\begin{equation*} a_{r}\hat{r} = \left( -a_{r} \right) \left( -\hat{r} \right). \end{equation*}
So the projection of \(\vec{a}\) along \(-\hat{r}\) is \(-a_{r}\text{.}\) According to Theorem 4.1.2 the displacement in the \(-\hat{r}\) direction is
\begin{equation*} \frac{1}{2} \left( - a_{r} \right) \left( \Delta t \right)^2. \end{equation*}
Now we can use the pythagorean theorem to solve for \(a_{r}\text{.}\)
\begin{gather*} \left[\frac{1}{2} a_{t} \left( \Delta t \right)^{2} + v \Delta t \right]^{2} + r^{2} = \left[-\frac{1}{2} a_{r} \left( \Delta t \right)^{2} + r \right]^{2} \\ \\ \left( \frac{a_{t}^{2}}{4} \right) \left( \Delta t \right)^{4} + a_{t} v \left( \Delta t \right)^{3} + v^{2} \left( \Delta t \right)^{2} + r^{2} = \left( \frac{a_{r}^{2}}{4} \right) \left( \Delta t \right)^{4} - a_{r} r \left( \Delta t \right)^{2} + r^{2}\\ \\ -a_{r} r \left( \Delta t \right)^{2} = \left( \frac{a_{t}^{2} - a_{r}^{2}}{4} \right) \left( \Delta t \right)^{4} + a_{t} v \left( \Delta t \right)^{3} + v^{2} \left( \Delta t \right)^{2}\\ \\ a_{r} = \left( \frac{a_{r}^{2} - a_{t}^{2}}{4r} \right) \left( \Delta t \right)^{2} - \frac{a_{t} v}{r} \Delta t - \frac{v^{2}}{r} \end{gather*}
We want to know the radial component of the instantaneous acceleration. To get the radial component of the acceleration at an instant we need \(\Delta t \rightarrow 0\text{.}\) Plugging in zero for \(\Delta t\) gives
\begin{equation*} a_{r} = - \frac{v^{2}}{r}. \end{equation*}
