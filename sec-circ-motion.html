    Section 18.2 Circular Motion

    Now we want to relate linear and angular kinematics for circular motion, but first we need to take a detour to further explore polar coordinates.
    🔗

    Subsection 18.2.1 Relating Cartesian and Polar Coordinate Systems

    Unit vectors point in the direction that increases the coordinate they’re associated but keeps the other coordinates constant. In the cartesian coordinate system the \(\hat{x}\) unit vector points in the direction that increases \(x\) but keeps \(y\) constant. The \(\hat{y}\) unit vector points in the direction that increases \(y\) but keeps \(x\) constant.
    🔗
    Cartesian coordinates are special because the unit vectors point in the same direction everywhere (They don’t depend on the the position \(\left( x, y \right)\)). Polar coordinates are defined by the radius \(r\) and angle \(\theta\) where \(\theta\) is in radians.
    🔗
    Unit vectors point in the direction that increases their coordinate but keeps the other coordinats constant. Therefore, the \(\hat{r}\) unit vector points in the direction that increases \(r\text{,}\) but keeps \(\theta\) constant. Likewise the \(\hat{\theta}\) unit vector points in the direction that increases \(\theta\text{,}\) but keeps \(r\) constant. The direction of unit vectors in polar coordinates depends on position \(\left( r, \theta \right)\text{.}\) The fact that unit vectors for polar coordinates depend on position is usually undesirable. Let’s express them in terms of cartesian unit vectors which don’t depend on position to deal with this.
    🔗
    By projecting the \(\hat{r}\) and \(\hat{\theta}\) unit vectors onto the \(\hat{x}\) and \(\hat{y}\) unit vectors we get the following equations:
    \begin{equation*} \hat{r} = \cos (\theta) \hat{x} + \sin (\theta) \hat{y} \end{equation*}
    \begin{equation*} \hat{\theta} = - \sin (\theta) \hat{x} + \cos (\theta) \hat{y}. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    🔗

    Subsection 18.2.2 Circular Motion

    Now we have the tools we need to relate linear and angular kinematics in circular motion.
    🔗

    Theorem 18.2.4.

    The average speed of an object undergoing circular motion is
    \begin{equation*} s_{avg} = r \omega_{z,avg} \end{equation*}
    🔗
    🔗

    Proof.

    From Definition 3.1.2 and equation (18.1.1) we have
    \begin{equation*} s_{avg} = \frac{d}{\Delta t} = \frac{r \Delta \theta_{z}}{\Delta t}. \end{equation*}
    Then by Definition 18.1.3 we have
    \begin{equation*} s_{avg} = r \omega_{z,avg}. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    🔗

    Theorem 18.2.5.

    The instantaneous speed of an object undergoing circular motion is
    \begin{equation*} v = r \omega_{z} \end{equation*}
    🔗
    🔗

    Proof.

    From the proof of Theorem 18.2.4, we have
    \begin{equation*} s_{avg} = r \omega_{z,avg} \end{equation*}
    If \(\Delta t\) in Definition 18.1.3 becomes extremely small compared to the objects velocity then we get a good approximation of the speed the object has at any given instant. If we call the instantaneous z-component of the angular velocity \(\omega_{z}\) and acknowledge that the instantaneous speed is the magnitude of velocity \(v\) we obtain the result we want.
    🔗
    🔗

    Theorem 18.2.6.

    The velocity of an object undergoing circular motion is
    \begin{equation*} \vec{v} = r \omega_{z} \hat{\theta} \end{equation*}
    🔗
    🔗

    Proof.

    We already know the magnitude of the velocity from Theorem 18.2.5, so we’ll focus on getting the direction at any instant. Since the object is undergoing circular motion, it is constrained to stay on a circle of constant radius \(r\text{.}\) Then at any instant, if \(\omega_{z}\) is positive it can only be moving in the direction that increases \(\theta\) but keeps \(r\) constant. As we discussed earlier in this section, \(\hat{\theta}\) is the unique unit vector pointing in this direction at any location on the circle.
    🔗
    🔗
    The easiest way to understand instantaneous acceleration of an object undergoing circular motion is to break it down into its \(r\) and \(\theta\) components. We can break the acceleration into its \(r\) and \(\theta\) components in exactly the same way we broke the acceleration down into its \(x\)- and \(y\)-components earlier in the course. the \(r\) component of acceleration is called the radial or centripedal component of acceleration \(a_{r}\text{.}\) centripedal is latin word that means center seeking. The \(\theta\) component of acceleration \(a_{t}\) is called the tangential component of acceleration becaue \(a_{t}\hat{\theta}\) always points in a direction that is tangent to the circle.
    🔗

    Theorem 18.2.7.

    For an object undergoing circular motion, the radial component of acceleration is
    \begin{equation*} a_{r} = - \frac{v^{2}}{r} \end{equation*}
    🔗
    🔗

    Proof.

    The acceleration of an object undergoing circular motion can be broken down into a tangential component \(a_{t}\) which is the projection of \(\vec{a}\) onto the \(\hat{\theta}\) direction and a radial component \(a_{r}\) which is the projection of \(\vec{a}\) onto the \(\hat{r}\) direction.
    🔗
    Figure x shows the change in position of an object undergoing circular motion over a very short period of time \(\Delta t\text{.}\) Since the period of time \(\Delta t\) is so small we can approximate the acceleration as constant over this interval. During that period of time the displacements in the \(\hat{\theta}\) and \(\hat{r}\) directions must add up to keep the object on the circle. \(\vec{v}\) and \(a_{t}\hat{\theta}\) point in the \(\hat{\theta}\) direction, so according to Theorem 4.1.2 the total displacment in the \(\hat{\theta}\) direction is
    \begin{equation*} \frac{1}{2}a_{t} \left( \Delta t \right)^{2} + v \Delta t. \end{equation*}
    Since the \(\hat{\theta}\) is tangent to the circle, this displacement tries to get the object to fly off the circle in a straight line.
    🔗
    To keep the object from flying off, we must have the radial displacement be in the \(-\hat{r}\) direction (towards the center of the circle) to keep the object on circle. \(a_{r}\) is the projection of \(\vec{a}\) unto the positive \(\hat{r}\) direction. To relate \(a_{r}\) to the projection of \(\vec{a}\) unto the \(-\hat{r}\) direct we note that
    \begin{equation*} a_{r}\hat{r} = \left( -a_{r} \right) \left( -\hat{r} \right). \end{equation*}
    So the projection of \(\vec{a}\) along \(-\hat{r}\) is \(-a_{r}\text{.}\) According to Theorem 4.1.2 the displacement in the \(-\hat{r}\) direction is
    \begin{equation*} \frac{1}{2} \left( - a_{r} \right) \left( \Delta t \right)^2. \end{equation*}
    Now we can use the pythagorean theorem to solve for \(a_{r}\text{.}\)
    \begin{gather*} \left[\frac{1}{2} a_{t} \left( \Delta t \right)^{2} + v \Delta t \right]^{2} + r^{2} = \left[-\frac{1}{2} a_{r} \left( \Delta t \right)^{2} + r \right]^{2} \\ \\ \left( \frac{a_{t}^{2}}{4} \right) \left( \Delta t \right)^{4} + a_{t} v \left( \Delta t \right)^{3} + v^{2} \left( \Delta t \right)^{2} + r^{2} = \left( \frac{a_{r}^{2}}{4} \right) \left( \Delta t \right)^{4} - a_{r} r \left( \Delta t \right)^{2} + r^{2}\\ \\ -a_{r} r \left( \Delta t \right)^{2} = \left( \frac{a_{t}^{2} - a_{r}^{2}}{4} \right) \left( \Delta t \right)^{4} + a_{t} v \left( \Delta t \right)^{3} + v^{2} \left( \Delta t \right)^{2}\\ \\ a_{r} = \left( \frac{a_{r}^{2} - a_{t}^{2}}{4r} \right) \left( \Delta t \right)^{2} - \frac{a_{t} v}{r} \Delta t - \frac{v^{2}}{r} \end{gather*}
    We want to know the radial component of the instantaneous acceleration. To get the radial component of the acceleration at an instant we need \(\Delta t \rightarrow 0\text{.}\) Plugging in zero for \(\Delta t\) gives
    \begin{equation*} a_{r} = - \frac{v^{2}}{r}. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    🔗

    Theorem 18.2.9.

    For an object undergoing circular motion, the tangential componenet of acceleration is
    \begin{equation*} a_{t} = r \alpha_{z} \end{equation*}
    🔗
    🔗

    Proof.

    The average tangential acceleration is given by
    \begin{equation*} a_{t,avg} = \frac{\Delta s}{\Delta t} \end{equation*}
    where \(\Delta s\) is the change in speed and \(\Delta t\) is the change in time. Plugging in the formula for the instantaneous speed from Theorem 18.2.5 gives
    \begin{equation*} a_{t,avg} = \frac{r \Delta \omega_{z}}{\Delta t}. \end{equation*}
    By definition Definition 18.1.4 we have
    \begin{equation*} a_{t,avg} = r \alpha_{z,avg}. \end{equation*}
    If \(\Delta t\) in Definition 18.1.4 becomes extremely small compared to the objects angular acceleration then we get a good approximation of the z-component of angular acceleration the object has at any given instant. If we call the instantaneous z-component of the angular acceleration \(\alpha_{z}\) and we call the instantaneous tangential acceleration \(a_{t}\) we obtain the result we want.
    🔗
    🔗
    Plugging Theorem 18.2.5 into Theorem 18.2.7 gives another useful formula for the radial component of the acceleration.
    \begin{align*} a_{r} \amp = - \frac{v^2}{r} \\ \\ \amp = - \frac{r^{2} \omega_{z}^{2}}{r} \\ \end{align*}
    \begin{equation} a_{r} = - r \omega_{z}^{2}\tag{18.2.1} \end{equation}
    Finally, the radial and tangential components of the acceleration are always orthogonal (at a right angle to each other) so we can use the pythagorean theorem to get
    \begin{equation*} \lvert \vec{a} \rvert = \sqrt{a_{t}^{2} + a_{r}^{2}.} \end{equation*}
